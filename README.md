## What Is the Best Way To Learn a Language? 
Okay, so like I said in the last section, I am an analyst and wanted to know the best way to learn the language. Then, thinking about my history and the personal opinions I have gained over the last six years, it hit me. Every way is the right way, but the wrong way every single time is to do it without enough volume. 

What do I mean by volume? Well, the "Learn To Lingo Method" will be explained next. 

### The Learn To Lingo Method (LTLM)
LTLM is a method anyone can use to learn a language, and the main premise for this method to work is VOLUME. Volume can be measured in many ways, so here are some examples: 
1. Time speaking
2. Time reading
3. Time writing
4. Time listening
5. Time studying grammar
6. A certain count of flashcards per day
7. A specific number of chapters read in a book
8. You get the point.

If you want to learn a language, the method you use to learn does not matter, but the volume and consistency of your study do matter. 

If you want to be good at speaking, you need to speak, but you need to speak A LOT (volume). It does not matter what you speak about, what you speak to, who you speak to, or how you speak, but the fact is that you are speaking. You do not need to spend a DOLLAR on learning, but in some cases it may make your life easier or your learning process faster. Not more effective, just faster to get your feet wet. 

I do not want you to get caught up in what video you are watching, the vocabulary it is teaching you, or what specific book you read vs. what someone else read. It literally does not matter. Volume matters. 

So that is our method. You need to do volume.

## Your Spanish Study Plan
This study plan is honestly for NEW beginners, like if you don't know a single word, and for people who are stuck at an A1-A2 level or maybe even B1 and who struggle with grammar. 

There is a strategy called spaced repetition. You can use spaced repetition software like an app you have probably heard of called [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/). Anki is an SRS, and study after study shows that it works, but you need to do it every single day and in volume!

There is another strategy that's backed by loads of studies called comprehensible input, as we explained earlier. That is usually done through immersion (another language learning method). Comprehensible input is where you understand about 70-90% of the content, and the rest is new. If you can get the gist of a story or what someone is saying, the rest can usually be understood thanks to how they are acting, the situation you are standing in, what's being pointed out in a video, etc. 

  Now there is something I want to mention about comprehensible input, especially when it goes side by side with immersion. Many people believe immersion should not involve your native tongue because they think reading a subtitle in English and then translating it into Spanish creates bad habits or reduces your ability to learn a language. Their goal is to avoid translating. For anyone over the age of 8-12 (who can read in their native tongue), that is simply not true. There are plenty of studies that show that when an individual is taught, in their native tongue, the meaning of a word translated into the language they want to know and then they are immersed in a way that uses what they were just taught, their total understanding of that missing 30%-10% dramatically increases. We are not babies. Everything is new for a baby, including grammar, reading, writing, listening, etc. This is your second time, so we can skip hundreds or thousands of hours trying to understand that stuff when someone can simply explain it to us real quick, and we are good to go. 

  Lastly, we want to study in a way that works our brains the hardest first and then study the easiest stuff last. 

  We all know it is super easy to glaze over when watching a video and realize we were not truly listening. Although that is true, I think listening to content through immersion that is comprehensible is okay to do last, even if you find yourself glazing over, because we hope your brain is still listening a bit to the sounds, even if you aren't understanding the words. 

  ### Your Daily Study Flow

  1. We want to start with Reading/Writing first. Reading takes a lot of brain capacity compared to listening. Studies show that in your native tongue, you never actually read the whole word. You usually read the first two - three letters and the last three or four, and your brain can tell what the word is. When learning a new language, you must read and sound out every letter to simply know how to pronounce it later and to learn the word! Always read aloud. This will help you catch yourself when you realize you are no longer paying attention or when a word sounds wrong. I often catch myself reading a word in my brain and saying, "IDK what that means!" Then I read it aloud and hear it, and if I have heard that word through video immersion, I go, "Oh, I know that word. It's blah blah blah."
  2. Writing. Journaling is usually the most common method here, but you have to sit there and, like when you're speaking, produce words! If you do not know how to say a word or a sentence, look it up online and write it out! I recommend [SpanishDictionary](https://www.spanishdict.com/) for your translations. The mind-to-hand coordination for writing is simply a deeper way of learning and will create a deep neurological connection to help you remember the words for later. 
  3. Flashcards/SRS. Lingvist specifically referenced some studies that showed that when someone learned something through SRS and was tested on it over 30 years, if I recall correctly, their retention of the information went up and up between years 7 and 17, and after 17 years of not recalling something, it started to drop off. What do I mean by this? When someone learned something through SRS, they actually did better on that same test at year 7 vs 1. Their brain simply put it somewhere better, but then after year 17, they started to forget what they didn't use. So what does this mean for you? It means if we learn something with SRS, we will probably remember it forever because I am going to assume that in a 7-17-year period, you are going to use your language to speak to SOMEONE.
  4. Lastly, listening. Many will argue this should be first or what you do the most of, so you can understand how the language sounds...and I do not disagree. However, when it comes down to human nature and what our brains can do day to day, we will almost always be able to turn on a Spanish video on YouTube at the end of our day, but we sure won't be able to read a chapter of a grammar book unless we are really motivated. Also, if you want to learn a language to communicate well while traveling, you really need to practice producing words and speaking phrases for your trip, not listen to thousands of hours of YouTube. 

  ## Your Study Guide Material
  Okay, so the goal is to try and use one of every single category each day! This is consistency through exposure to all methods (reading, writing, listening, speaking). When you do each one of these methods each day, you become more well-rounded, and you are actually doing SRS. You may read the Spanish word "casi," which means "almost" in English. Then you are writing in your journal, and you say, "I almost got hit by a car today while getting off the train." In Spanish, that's "Hoy casi me atropella un coche al bajarme del tren." Look at that, the word "casi" appears! That was spaced repetition between two study moments. Then someone in a YouTube video says, "You almost bought that car but could not afford it," or in Spanish, "Casi compraste ese coche, pero no podías permitírtelo." You get the point.
